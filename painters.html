The painters

Who came, and who brought them

Cape Ann’s painters arrived by invitation more often than by chance. A teacher brought his class, a friend found a cottage, an older painter asked two young ones to come for the summer. These are their lives on Cape Ann, and the chains that brought them.

Fitz Henry Lane

1804–1865 · Born in Gloucester; painted it 1840s–1865

Gloucester’s own painter learned his trade in the lithography shops before turning to oil, and came home to paint the harbor for two decades1. The Cape Ann Museum holds 40 of his paintings, his first known work (a watercolor of 1830), about 100 drawings and all three of his lithographed views of the town12. He worked from careful measured drawings, then painted several canvases from each; see site 4.

  • Gloucester HarborFitz Henry Lane, 1852Cape Ann Museum, Gloucester
  • Brace's Rock, Eastern Point, GloucesterFitz Henry Lane, c. 1864National Gallery of Art, Washington

Fitz Henry Lane and Mary Blood Mellen: all works

Mary Blood Mellen

1819–1886 · Working with Lane from the mid-1850s

Born in Vermont and schooled in watercolor at a girls’ academy, Mellen married a Universalist minister in 1840. When his brother took the Gloucester pulpit in 1855, she came into Lane’s orbit and was soon working beside him, free to copy his drawings and sometimes his canvases. One painting carries both signatures. The museum notes she is often called a “copyist”, and that Lane plainly respected her work1.

William Morris Hunt

1824–1879 · Summer studio at Magnolia, 1870s

Hunt brought the French Barbizon painters’ ideas to Boston and taught John La Farge and both William and Henry James. His summer studio in Magnolia drew other artists to Cape Ann, and from 1868 he opened his classes to women, many from Boston’s leading families13.

  • Gloucester HarborWilliam Morris Hunt, about 1877Museum of Fine Arts, Boston
  • Sand Bank with Willows, MagnoliaWilliam Morris Hunt, 1877The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York

William Morris Hunt and Alfred T. Bricher: all works

Winslow Homer

1836–1910 · Summers of 1873 and 1880

Homer left the studio in Gloucester. In 1873 he painted outdoors and made himself a master of watercolor, almost to the exclusion of oil; in 1880 he boarded on Ten Pound Island in the middle of the harbor14. Both summers are on the guide’s map as sites 1 and 2.

  • Breezing Up (A Fair Wind)Winslow Homer, 1873–1876National Gallery of Art, Washington
  • Eastern Point LightWinslow Homer, 1880Princeton University Art Museum

Frank Duveneck

1848–1919 · About fifteen summers from 1890

In 1890 the Kentucky-born painter brought a summer class of students from Boston and the Cincinnati Academy, apparently the first large class of its kind in town, and he went on to paint about a hundred Gloucester pictures15. He drew others after him, among them Theodore Wendel, Joseph DeCamp, John W. Alexander and John Twachtman. He is said to have kept two studios, one for the morning light and one for the afternoon2.

  • Horizon at GloucesterFrank Duveneck, c. 1905Cape Ann Museum, Gloucester
  • Study of Braces Rock, GloucesterFrank Duveneck, c. 1893Cape Ann Museum, Gloucester

Metcalf, Duveneck, Beaux, Bolton Jones, Whitman, Wendel and Gifford: all works

John H. Twachtman

1853–1902 · Summers of 1900–1902, East Gloucester

Twachtman spent his last summers at the Harbor View Hotel in East Gloucester, painting the boats and wharves. His view of the hotel from the end of its pier was on his easel when he died there in August 190216; Duveneck had been painting beside him only days before15. Through his pupil Leon Kroll, his influence reached Edward Hopper3.

  • Fishing Boats at GloucesterJohn H. Twachtman, 1901Smithsonian American Art Museum, Washington

Cecilia Beaux

1855–1942 · Eastern Point, mid-1880s–1942

Philadelphia’s great portraitist, and from 1895 to 1915 the first woman to teach at the Pennsylvania Academy, Beaux first came with her family in the mid-1880s and stayed at the Fairview Inn. Fame cost her privacy, so in 1903 she rented a cottage on Eastern Point and by 1906 had built Green Alley, her house and studio there. Her 1930 memoir, Background with Figures, credits her family for planting what grew for her in Gloucester17.

  • Half-Tide, Annisquam RiverCecilia Beaux, ca. 1905High Museum of Art, Atlanta

Maurice Prendergast

1858–1924 · About 1907–1923

Prendergast made more than 2,300 paintings and drawings, many of them New England beaches. In his Gloucester watercolors a loose pencil sketch guided rather than governed the brush: he changed his mind as he went, so that figures turned into rocks, and he sharpened edges with touches of pastel18.

Leon Kroll

1884–1974 · 1912; Folly Cove from the 1930s

Twachtman’s student at the Art Students League, Kroll found Cézanne in Paris and showed at the 1913 Armory Show3. He brought Edward Hopper to Gloucester in 19124. From the 1930s he and his wife Genevieve summered at Folly Cove near Halibut Point, bought a house there in 1944, and counted Paul Manship, Walker Hancock and Virginia Lee Burton among their friends3. He also painted the Bass Rocks footbridge that Rothko later sketched19.

Edward Hopper

1882–1967 · 1912, 1923, 1924, 1926 and 1928

Hopper’s 1912 oils are solid and dark. In 1923 he courted Josephine Nivison here and, at her urging, took up watercolor; the next summer the newlyweds came back on an extended honeymoon, exploring by foot and streetcar4. By 1928 his Gloucester sales had bought him a car20. The Hopper trail finds twelve of his subjects.

John Sloan

1871–1951 · Summers of 1914–1918

After the 1913 Armory Show, the Ashcan painter went looking for new ground. His friend Charles Winter urged Gloucester, and from 1914 Sloan rented a little red cottage at the head of Rocky Neck for five summers. It filled with painters: Stuart Davis, Agnes Richmond, Charles and Alice Beach Winter, Paul Cornoyer, Paul Tietjens. The Cape Ann Museum owns eight of his Gloucester pictures5. The cottage still stands at 252 East Main Street8.

Hopper, Sloan, Davis, Demuth, Hartley and others: all works

Stuart Davis

1892–1964 · Summers of 1915–1934

Sloan invited him in 1915, and Davis called Gloucester “the place I had been looking for”. He came back for many summers through 1934, drawn to the schooners and the clutter of the docks. In time he left the easel and canvases behind and went out with only a sketchbook and pen, and in Gloucester he began combining several views and moments in one picture, the Cubist idea that runs through his later work6.

Hopper, Sloan, Davis, Demuth, Hartley and others: all works

Charles Demuth

1883–1935 · Summer of 1919

In 1919 Demuth painted Gloucester’s masts, roofs and church towers outdoors, cutting them into the ruled, transparent planes that became Precisionism21. The RISD Museum’s Gloucester has released its image to the public domain22.

  • GloucesterCharles Demuth, 1919RISD Museum, Providence
  • Box of TricksCharles Demuth, 1919Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts, Philadelphia

Marsden Hartley

1877–1943 · 1920, 1931 and 1934

Hartley shunned the busy waterfront for Dogtown, the abandoned colonial settlement in the middle of Cape Ann, which he thought looked like a cross between Easter Island and Stonehenge. He wrote poems on the backs of some of the paintings23. In 1931 he boarded with the Wilkens family at 9 Rocky Neck Avenue, treating the summer as a warm-up for a Guggenheim-funded trip to Mexico8.

Hopper, Sloan, Davis, Demuth, Hartley and others: all works

Theresa Bernstein and William Meyerowitz

1890–2002 · 1887–1981 · Summers from 1916 and 1919

Bernstein first came in 1916 on a friend’s advice. Meyerowitz, born in Russia, had paid for his training by singing in the Metropolitan Opera; the two met through the People’s Art Guild and married in 191924. In the early 1920s Ellen Day Hale lent them a Folly Cove cottage, and Bernstein’s New England Ladies (1925) records an elderberry-wine party there, guest by guest7. Meyerowitz’s Gloucester Humoresque marks the founding in 1922 of the North Shore Arts Association, which juried its shows, and the Gloucester Society of Artists, which proudly did not24.

Milton Avery

1885–1965 · Most summers from 1920 to 1945

Avery’s first Gloucester pictures still owe something to Twachtman; over his Cape Ann summers the forms flattened and the color took over. He held to one rule: simplify anything, invent nothing. In 1932 he brought his young friends Gottlieb, Rothko and Barnett Newman10. The museum dates his first visit to 1920; the Rocky Neck Art Colony says 19219.

  • At the museum
    Gloucester DawnMilton Avery, c. 1921Frances Lehman Loeb Art Center, Vassar College

Milton Avery, Adolph Gottlieb and Mark Rothko: all works

Mark Rothko

1903–1970 · 1932, 1934 and 1945

In 1934 Rothko and his wife, Edith Sachar, reached Gloucester before the Gottliebs and took the Calder Street house the Gottliebs had wanted25. He painted the fish wharfs from Banner Hill, the bathers, and his friends at handball; the catalogue raisonné lists 38 Gloucester sheets26. In 1965 he gave the memorial address for Avery, calling him above all a great poet10. The Rothko trail has every sheet you can see.

Emile Gruppé

1896–1978 · Rocky Neck from 1929

Gruppé opened the Gloucester School of Art on Rocky Neck in 1929 and ran it for thirty years. His advice to students: think of a shape first, not of a horse or a tree or a boat27.

  • At the museum
    Gloucester DraggersEmile Gruppé, n.d. (artist list gives ca. 1942)New Britain Museum of American Art

Nell Blaine

1922–1996 · 1943; Rocky Neck 1954 and 1958; East Gloucester 1974–1996

A Hans Hofmann student and a founder of New York’s Jane Street Gallery, Blaine first came in 1943 and rented Rocky Neck studios in 1954 and 1958. Time named her one of five leading women artists in America in 1957. Two years later polio left her in a wheelchair; she learned to paint again, and from 1974 summered at Eudora Cottage in East Gloucester, painting her garden, the ledges and the harbor28.
