Layer · the harbor at work

The working waterfront

The harbor in the paintings was a factory. This page explains what the painters were looking at — fish flakes, dories, schooners, nets — and where the working waterfront can still be seen.

Harbor words

A glossary of the working harbor, from the vessels and their rigging to the cure, the pay and the customs, with a source for every word. Terms with a picture can be seen in paintings on this site.

Vessels

Schooners

The fore-and-aft rigged vessels of the Gloucester fleet that brought fish home from the banks1.

  • Gloucester Schooners and WaterboatWinslow Homer, 1880Philadelphia Museum of Art
  • Gloucester HarborFitz Henry Lane, 1852Cape Ann Museum, Gloucester
Draggers

Motor trawlers that drag a net along the bottom; Emile Gruppé painted them at the wharves.

  • At the museum
    Gloucester DraggersEmile Gruppé, n.d. (artist list gives ca. 1942)New Britain Museum of American Art
Shallop

A small colonial working boat, the ancestor of many New England fishing craft, among them the Chebacco boat, the dogbody and the pinky2.

Chebacco boat

A heavily built two-masted fishing boat that grew out of the shallop in Chebacco Parish, the part of Ipswich that became the town of Essex in 1819. The name was in use in the second half of the 1700s2,3.

Dogbody

The square-sterned version of the Chebacco boat. Where the name came from is now forgotten2.

Pinky

A schooner-rigged fishing vessel that descended from the Chebacco boat at the end of the 1700s. The name refers to its narrow “pink” stern, from a Dutch word for pinched2,4.

Jigger

A Chebacco boat converted to a schooner rig by adding a bowsprit and a jib5. Gloucester’s fishing timeline records jiggers of 30 tons and more built for mackerel fishing in 18166.

Sharpshooter

The new fishing schooner of the 1840s: a long hull, a straight keel, sharp bilges and the first hint of a hollow, slicing bow3. Lane’s Ships in Ice off Ten Pound Island shows fishing schooners with the finer hulls of the sharpshooters and clippers7.

  • Ships in Ice off Ten Pound Island, GloucesterFitz Henry Lane, 1850sMuseum of Fine Arts, Boston
Clipper schooner

A fast fishing schooner: sharp-ended, shallow, beamy and over-canvassed. The first clipper owned in Gloucester was built at Essex in 18506; the Smuggler of 1877 was one3.

Plumb-stem schooner

Dennison J. Lawlor’s design, with an upright stem and a deeper, more stable hull than the clippers. The Fish Commission’s research schooner Grampus, launched in 1885, helped set the new pattern3.

Fredonia schooner

Schooners named after their prototype, the Fredonia: a graceful bow, a roomier foredeck and the rounded stern typical of the Gloucesterman3.

Knockabout

A schooner with no bowsprit8. The first was built in Essex in 1902 to make the work safer for fishermen9; Joseph Garland counts the type among the last of the classic Gloucester schooners3.

Gloucesterman

The classic Gloucester fishing schooner of the late 1800s. Built for a hard-fought trade without engines, it was sailed hard, because speed on the voyage home meant success10.

Tern

A schooner with three masts8.

Georgesman

A schooner that fished Georges Bank. Gloucester was using the name by the 1850s6.

Salt banker

A schooner that sailed from Gloucester to the Grand Banks of Newfoundland for cod. Kipling’s Captains Courageous (1897) gives an accurate picture of their life11.

Auxiliary

A sailing vessel with an engine as well. Gloucester’s first fishing vessel with an auxiliary engine came in 1900, and in the 1920s the fleet began converting to engines, mostly diesel9.

Coasting schooner

Any merchant schooner carrying cargo from one port on the coast to another12.

Packet

A vessel that carried passengers and higher-value goods between particular ports on a regular schedule. The packet sloops were the most elegant12.

Stone sloop

A heavily built, undecorated sloop that carried granite blocks from the quarry piers around Cape Ann to other ports2.

Half brig

The New England name for the hermaphrodite brig: square sails on the foremast only, and a spanker and gaff topsail on the main. It was the commonest brig in the coasting trade2.

Salt ship

A ship or bark that brought salt to Gloucester for curing fish. Too deep to reach the wharves fully loaded, it was partly unloaded at anchor first13.

Lighter

A small vessel that takes cargo off a deep-loaded ship so that she can reach a wharf. Gloucester did much of this “lightering” in the Stream13,4.

Yawl boat

A schooner’s own boat, usually twenty feet long or less, round-bottomed and square-sterned, hung in davits at the stern12. It was the lifeboat at sea and carried the captain and crew ashore in port5. Lane painted one frozen into the harbor ice7.

  • Ships in Ice off Ten Pound Island, GloucesterFitz Henry Lane, 1850sMuseum of Fine Arts, Boston
Moses boat

A boat carried in a schooner’s stern davits and used to bring barrels of rum and molasses out from a beach13.

Wherry

A larger, wider relative of the dory, used for bulky coastal gear such as gill nets and fish traps14.

Seine boat

The big rowing boat used in seining. During St. Peter’s Fiesta, crews still race seine boats a half mile out from Pavilion Beach and back15.

Rig and deck

Bowsprit

The long spar at the bow that carries the headsails out over the water. Sailors called it the widow-maker, because men were lost falling from it while tending sail8.

Gaff

On a schooner, the spar along the head of a four-sided sail8. In a dory, a gaff was the hook carried for bringing fish aboard16.

Foremast and mainmast

A schooner has at least two masts: the foremast, nearest the bow, and the mainmast behind it, the taller of the two8.

Topmast

A second spar carried on top of a lower mast, used to fly more sail8.

Jumbo

The larger of a schooner’s headsails, set forward of the foremast with the jib8.

Yankee

A headsail flown above and forward of the jib, usually on vessels with a bowsprit8.

Queen topsail

A small staysail set between the foremast and the mainmast8.

Halyards

The lines that hoist the sails, and the gaffs and booms that hold them8.

Lazyjacks

Lines from the topping lifts to under the boom that catch the sail like a net when it is lowered8.

Baggywrinkle

Clumps of frayed rope tied into the rigging so that the sails do not chafe against the lines8.

Hanging in the gear

Sails partly lowered or clewed up to dry, as Lane drew them on a half brig in 18512.

Crosstrees

Horizontal timbers high on the mast that spread the shrouds8.

Davits

Small cranes, usually at the stern, for raising and lowering a boat8. Banks schooners carried their yawl boats in stern davits5.

Scuppers

Holes in the vessel’s sides at deck level that drain water overboard8.

Fo’c’sle

Short for forecastle: the crew’s quarters in the forward part of the hull8.

Charley Noble

The stovepipe of the galley, the ship’s kitchen8.

Pilothouse

A small cabin on deck that shelters the wheel and the helmsman8.

Iron Mike

Fishermen’s name for the autopilot, the electrical device that holds a vessel on a set course17.

Gypsyhead

A metal drum with a smooth, curved face, usually mounted on a winch17.

Slaughterhouse

The square compartments of the fish hold along the centerline over the keel, between the pens on either side17.

Pen boards

Removable dividers of wood or metal that slot into the hold to make pens; when all are in, the hold is “penned up”17. Dorymen used smaller ones to section off the dory for fish18.

The dory and its gear

Dories

Small flat-bottomed boats carried on the schooners and rowed out to set lines and nets; Gordon Parks photographed Gloucester crews laying nets by hand from them in 194319. Homer drew boys in dories in 1873.

  • Boys in a DoryWinslow Homer, 1873The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
  • The Green DoryWinslow Homer, 1880Museum of Fine Arts, Boston
Doryman

One of the two men who fished from a dory18.

Dory trawling

Fishing long trawl lines of baited hooks from six to ten dories carried on a schooner. The Gloucester schooner Anna made a successful dory-trawling trip to the Grand Banks in 1854, but Gloucester was slow to take up the method14. Gloucester’s timeline marks 1941 as the year draggers replaced dory fishing9.

Trawl

A long ground line, buoyed and anchored, hung with many short lines of baited hooks18. In 1881 a tub of haddock trawl held 500 hooks on about 292 fathoms of line16.

Trawl tub

A half-barrel holding a coiled trawl and its hooks18. In 1881 a dory set out with six to eight of them16.

Slivers

Strips of salted menhaden, the main bait for the winter haddock trawls of 1881; when menhaden ran short, fresh herring took its place. Baiting a tub of 500 hooks took 45 minutes to an hour16.

Dory anchor

A small iron anchor that holds one end of a trawl to the bottom18.

Buoy and black ball

The float marking a trawl: a numbered wooden barrel with a flag18. In 1881 each end buoy also carried a black ball16.

Gurdy

A hand crank fitted across the bow of a dory to haul in lines heavy with fish18.

Nippers

Doughnut-shaped grips that protected a fisherman’s fingers from the running trawl line18. In 1881 each dory carried two pairs, made of wool16.

Gob stick

A heavy two-pronged wooden stick for stunning fish and taking hooks from their throats18.

Halibut killer

A heavy wooden club for stunning or killing big fish such as halibut, which can weigh more than 200 pounds18.

Bailer

A wooden or metal scoop for throwing water out of the dory18.

Tholepins

Narrow wooden pegs set in a dory’s rail that hold the oars in place while rowing18.

Fog horn

Dorymen carried a fog horn and a kerosene lamp to signal the schooner, by sound or by light, in thick fog or darkness18.

Tombstone

The dory’s narrow, V-shaped stern board14.

Pay out

To feed a line over the side, hand over hand8.

Grounds, lines and nets

Gill nets

Nets hung like a curtain in the water, catching fish by the gills. Mellen’s Gloucester Harbor (Laying Nets) shows crews setting them20.

  • Gloucester Harbor (Laying Nets)Mary Blood Mellen, about 1870Bowdoin College Museum of Art, Brunswick, Maine
Hand-lining

Fishing with a single line carrying one or two hooks. Dory trawling, with many hooks on one line, later replaced it13.

Sling ding

A metal rod that held the two hooks of a Georges Bank hand line 12 to 18 inches apart13.

Highliner

The top boat, or the top fisherman, of the fleet. The word goes back to fishing from the deck, when the best fisherman held the highest place in the bow8.

Purse seine

A net about 1,000 feet long set in a circle around a school of mackerel. It replaced hand-lining for mackerel13; Gloucester’s timeline dates it to 18486.

Otter trawl

A net towed along the bottom and held open by two otter doors17. Its arrival in 1905 changed fishing on the New England banks9.

Footrope and headrope

The bottom and top lines of a trawl net. Floats of metal, plastic or glass on the headrope hold the mouth of the net open17.

Warps

The cables from a trawler’s winches to the two sides of the net, which carry the pull of the boat to the trawl17.

Georges

Fishermen’s short name for Georges Bank. The Fish Commission’s reports from Gloucester in 1883 list the cod and halibut landed “from George’s Bank” month by month21.

Grand Banks

The great fishing banks off Newfoundland, where Gloucester’s salt bankers went for cod11.

Fare

A vessel’s catch as landed. A report from Gloucester in December 1883 notes that the vessels from Georges Bank “all brought in good fares of cod”21.

Trip

Both the voyage and the fish it brings home: the whole catch aboard is “the trip”17.

Home run

The passage from the fishing grounds back to the home port17.

Shore fishery

Fishing near home in small vessels and boats. In November 1883 Gloucester’s shore fisheries had 56 vessels and 448 men21.

Groundfish

Fish that live near the bottom, such as cod and haddock, the mainstay of the New England fisheries11.

Ice

Packing fish in ice instead of salt became common about 1845, so that fresh fish could reach market6. With faster schooners and the railroad, it revived Gloucester’s fishing13. The ice was cut from fresh-water ponds11.

Fast freezing

Clarence Birdseye’s quick-freezing method, which in 1924 started the processing of frozen fish9.

Lobster trap

A baited trap set on the bottom, one of the traps of the coastal fisheries in Lane’s time14. Homer painted three boys in a dory with lobster pots in 1875.

  • Three Boys in a Dory with Lobster PotsWinslow Homer, 1875Nelson-Atkins Museum of Art, Kansas City

The catch and the cure

Fish flakes

Low wooden racks on which split, salted cod was laid out in the sun to dry. Whole fields of them appear in Lane’s and later paintings of the waterfront22,1.

  • At the museum
    Untitled (wharf, Gloucester, Massachusetts)Mark Rothko, 1934Cape Ann Museum, Gloucester
Flake yard

A field of fish flakes beside the wharves. Lane’s 1836 view of Gloucester shows the Wonson family’s flake yard in East Gloucester, and Five Pound Island later became one4.

Dressing gang

The crew that cleaned cod on deck: the throater cut down the belly and took off the head, the gutter removed the insides but saved the liver, and the splitter took out the backbone23.

Gurry

The slime, blood and fluids spilled when fish are dressed17. Schooners kept a gurry pen on deck16.

Kench cure

A salt cure that used more salt than dunfish but less than pickled “green” cod23.

Dunfish

Cod quickly dried and lightly salted, sometimes cured in dark rooms for two or three months under salt hay or eelgrass and weights. The name belongs to the small-boat fisheries of eastern Maine and the Bay of Fundy23.

Green cod

Cod pickled in casks of brine rather than dried; the cure took more salt than kenching23.

Tongues

Fishermen cut the tongue out of each cod they hauled aboard to keep count of their catch before the fish was salted24.

Codfish bounty

A federal payment that helped Gloucester fishermen rebuild their fleet after independence13.

Surinam trade

Gloucester’s trade with Surinam, on the north coast of South America, was under way by 1790 and paid for many of the town’s merchant vessels and wharves until it collapsed in 186313.

Lumper

A dock worker who unloads vessels in Gloucester Harbor25.

Fish pier

The Jodrey State Fish Pier, opened in 1938 and named for Everett R. Jodrey26.

Wharves and the harbor

Drydock

A berth where a vessel is taken out of the water for repair. Hassam painted one in the 1890s.

  • Drydock, GloucesterChilde Hassam, c. 1890sThe Phillips Collection, Washington
Crib wharf

A wharf built on cribs, rectangular frames of timber that look like a log cabin, filled in, with beams laid over the top crib to carry the deck13.

Cob wharf

A pier of piles and timber filled in with rocks. Gloucester built finger piers and bulkheads this way13.

Spiles

Gloucester’s word for the pilings driven along a wharf as fenders where vessels tie up13.

Rafting

Wharf space in Gloucester Harbor was scarce, so coasting vessels moored together and passed cargo to smaller craft that carried it to nearby towns, and the reverse2.

The Stream

The wide channel at the entrance to the Inner Harbor, where vessels anchored while they waited to unload, often lightering there first4.

Marine railway

A cradle on rails that hauls a vessel out of the water for repair. The Rocky Neck railways, begun in 1859 and worked by a steam engine until about 1970, are said to be the oldest still operating in the country27.

The Cut

The Blynman Canal, dug in 1643 between the harbor and the Annisquam River so that small vessels could avoid the passage around Cape Ann. Storms filled it with sand in 1704 and 1723; it was reopened in 182328.

Dog Bar Breakwater

The breakwater at Eastern Point that has kept southerly swells out of the harbor since 190529. Stay off it when it is wet or icy.

Five Pound Island

A small island in the harbor, once fit only for sheep pounds, that later became a flake yard for drying fish4.

Essex-built

Many Gloucester schooners came from the shipyards on the Essex River11. The first knockabout was built in Essex in 1902 and the Adventure in 19269; Arthur D. Story launched the Gertrude L. Thebaud there in 193030.

Life and pay aboard

Lay

The formal word for the share system, under which the money from a trip is divided between the boat and the crew17.

Settling up

Adding up the trip’s bills and working out each man’s share and the boat’s; the balance sheet is the settlement17.

Gross stock and net stock

The gross stock is all the money the boat and crew earned from a trip; the net stock is what is left after the bills charged against it are taken out17.

In the hole

When a trip earned less than its expenses, the crew went in the hole and paid the bills from their own pockets. The deficit was the hole bill17.

Delegate

The crewman elected to speak for the crew when the settlement is made17.

Site

An East Coast word for a berth on a fishing boat, or the promise of one17.

Mug-up

A snack between meals: a cup of coffee and a sandwich17.

Sou’wester

A wide-brimmed, waterproof oiled hat for foul weather17.

Wristlets

Cloth tubes worn on the forearms and held by a thumb loop. They keep the arms warm and wash clean of slime and blood more easily than sleeves17.

Ditty bag

A small bag for carrying and stowing a sailor’s personal things8.

Customs, races and memory

St. Peter’s Fiesta

Gloucester’s June festival for St. Peter, patron saint of fishermen, first held in 1926 and put on by the Italian-American community: an open-air Mass, processions, seine-boat races, the greasy pole and the blessing of the fleet9,31.

Greasy pole

A heavily greased pole run out over the harbor with a flag at the end, which walkers try to grab before they fall in. It was added to the Fiesta in 1931, after a contest in Terrasini, Sicily, the home town of many Gloucester families32,31.

Viva San Pietro!

“Long live St. Peter!” The cry is shouted in the Fiesta processions and by the greasy-pole walkers15.

Blessing of the fleet

On Fiesta Sunday a priest reads out the names of Gloucester’s fishing boats and blesses them33. Gloucester’s timeline dates the first blessing to 19459. In 1957 the Portuguese and Italian fleets were blessed on separate Sundays; by 1977 the two communities celebrated together34.

Fishermen’s Memorial Service

Each August a procession goes to the Man at the Wheel, where families lay oars and wreaths and cast flowers into the harbor for the men lost at sea35,36.

Cenotaph

The plaques beside the Man at the Wheel bearing the names of Gloucester fishermen lost at sea, more than 5,000 of them35. New names are still added36.

Man at the Wheel

Leonard Craske’s bronze helmsman on Stacy Boulevard, dedicated in 192537, with the line from Psalm 107, “They that go down to the sea in ships”38.

Our Lady of Good Voyage

The church of Gloucester’s Portuguese community, modeled on Santa Maria Madalena in the Azores. Between its towers a statue of Mary holds a two-masted schooner38.

Fishermen’s Wives Memorial

The memorial near the Man at the Wheel that honors the women whose faith kept fishing families going38.

International Fishermen’s Races

Races between fishing schooners of Gloucester and Nova Scotia, begun in 19209. Lunenburg’s Bluenose never lost a series39. The last, sailed off Gloucester and Boston in October 1938 against the Gertrude L. Thebaud, was the final race between North Atlantic sailing fishing schooners30.
