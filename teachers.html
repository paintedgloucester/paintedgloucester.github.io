For teachers

Three lessons on painting Cape Ann

Three lessons that use the guide’s paintings, sites and sun planner, written for grades 3–4, grades 7–8 and high school Foundations, and keyed to the 2019 Massachusetts Arts Curriculum Framework1.

Free to use and adapt in class under CC BY 4.0. Print this page from the browser: the header, buttons and footer drop out.

Grades 3–4 · Two 45-minute sessions

Same place, two painters

Goal. Students compare two paintings of one place and say how each painter used color and line to show a mood.

Standards addressed, from the 2019 Massachusetts Arts Curriculum Framework2
CodeStandard
3-4.V.R.07Perceive and analyze artistic work: how color, form, line, shape and texture show intent
3-4.V.R.08Interpret intent and meaning: describe contrasting interpretations
3-4.V.Cr.01Generate artistic ideas that explore line, shape and color

Paintings

  • Gloucester HarborWillard Metcalf, 1895Mead Art Museum, Amherst College
  • Gloucester HarborChilde Hassam, c. 1899Norton Museum of Art, West Palm Beach

Steps

  1. Project the two Banner Hill views of the harbor, painted by Willard Metcalf in 1895 and Childe Hassam in 1899 from the same hill (site 3).
  2. In pairs, list three colors each painter used most. Use the guide’s color lab to sample them.
  3. Ask: which painting feels warmer? Busier? Which time of day is it? Point to the evidence.
  4. Each student paints the same view from a photograph or the window in a different mood, using only three colors.

AssessmentEach student names one choice in their painting and the mood it creates.

Grades 7–8 · Three 50-minute sessions

Where did they stand?

Goal. Students use a painting, a map and the sun to find a painter’s viewpoint and explain what the painting shows about Gloucester’s history.

Standards addressed, from the 2019 Massachusetts Arts Curriculum Framework3
CodeStandard
7-8.V.R.07Analyze elements of a work that indicate its historical or cultural context
7-8.V.R.08Explain how a work was influenced by its culture or historical context
7-8.V.Co.11Relate artistic ideas and works to societal, cultural and historical contexts

Paintings

  • Ten Pound Island, GloucesterFitz Henry Lane, 1850sCape Ann Museum, Gloucester
  • At the museum
    The Mansard RoofEdward Hopper, 1923Brooklyn Museum

Steps

  1. Give groups one site page each. From the painting, the map and the direction of view, mark where the painter stood.
  2. Use the sun planner to find when the shadows in the painting would fall the way they do. Is the painter’s light morning or evening?
  3. List what is in the painting that is gone today (wharves, fish flakes, schooners). Use the working-waterfront page to explain each.
  4. Each group presents: what the painting shows about how people lived and worked when it was made.

AssessmentA one-page site report with the viewpoint, the time of day and two pieces of historical evidence from the painting.

High school Foundations · Four 55-minute sessions

From documentary to abstraction

Goal. Students trace how Gloucester’s wharves moved artists from description toward abstraction, then make a work that does the same.

Standards addressed, from the 2019 Massachusetts Arts Curriculum Framework4
CodeStandard
F.V.R.07Analyze the style of an artist and how it shows in a given artwork
F.V.Co.11Connect historical and cultural contexts to the stylistic elements of artistic movements
F.V.Cr.02Develop multiple plans for an artwork before selecting one

Paintings

  • At the museum
    Untitled (wharf, Gloucester, Massachusetts)Mark Rothko, 1934National Gallery of Art, Washington
  • At the museum
    Gloucester HarborStuart Davis, 1938Museum of Fine Arts, Houston

Steps

  1. Look at Lane’s harbor, Hopper’s houses, Rothko’s 1934 wharf and Siskind’s 1944 wharf photographs (linked on the photographers page). Arrange them on a line from “describes” to “abstracts”.
  2. Read the Siskind paragraph on the photographers page. What did Gloucester give these artists that New York did not?
  3. Photograph or sketch six close-up details of a real wall, boat or dock. Develop three plans for a composition from them.
  4. Make the final work and write a 150-word statement placing it on the line.

AssessmentThree dated plans, the finished work and the statement, judged on how clearly the student can explain the move from subject to abstraction.

Before a field trip

  • Check the access notes on each site page: several historic viewpoints are on private land.
  • Good Harbor and Wingaersheek beaches charge for parking in summer, and non-residents must reserve.
  • Ask the Cape Ann Museum about school visits before planning a trip around its galleries.
  • Mind the tide: the harbor range averages nearly 9 feet. Keep students off wet ledges.
