The photographers

Cape Ann was photographed by some of the century’s best: the government’s documentary photographers in the 1930s and 1940s, and Aaron Siskind, who found abstraction on its wharves. None of these photographs is reproduced here; each link goes to the museum or library that holds it.

Aaron Siskind

1903–1991 · Summers of 1944 and 1945, East Gloucester

Siskind came in July 1944 to a studio Adolph Gottlieb had found for him, and spent his mornings on the wharves and beaches with a camera and tripod. In Gloucester he turned from documentary work to close, flat views of peeling walls, rope, a discarded glove and granite. His painter friends Gottlieb, Barnett Newman and Mark Rothko encouraged him1. The curator Jan Howard called these summers his breakthrough1.

Gordon Parks

1912–2006 · 1943, for the Office of War Information

Parks photographed Gloucester’s fishing families at home and went to sea with the crew of the Alden, who laid their nets by hand from dories. The assignment was part of a wartime project on food production2. The photographs are in the Library of Congress.

Arthur Rothstein

1915–1985 · 1937, for the Farm Security Administration

Rothstein photographed the fishing boats and docks in the fall of 1937; prints are in the New York Public Library and the Library of Congress3.

Lee Friedlander

born 1934 · 1981

Friedlander photographed the city in 1981; the Museum of Modern Art owns Gloucester, Massachusetts of that year4.

Carl Chiarenza

born 1935 · 1962

Chiarenza, later Siskind’s biographer, photographed in Gloucester as a young man; the Whitney owns his Gloucester Window, Sailing Ship of 19625.
